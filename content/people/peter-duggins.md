I am interested in how networks of spiking neurons implement the computations behind learning and decision making. I study these topics by building functional brain models from biologically detailed components; my models emphasize anatomical plausibility alongside functional capacity, and I strive to test them against both behavioral and neural data.

During my PhD (Chris Eliasmith @ University of Waterloo), I investigated working memory and its pharmacological modulation in cortex, fear conditioning and extinction in the amygdala, and decision making subject to the speed–accuracy trade-off. During my postdoc at the CCNL (Alireza Soltani @ Dartmouth College), I have collaborated more closely with experimental psychologists and cognitive neuroscientists. Using a combination of behavioral experiments, neural recordings, and spiking neural network models, I studied the mechanisms underlying individual differences in how people integrate noisy evidence, as well as how monkeys arbitrate between multiple features during dynamic and uncertain learning tasks.

My current research asks how people learn and decide in complex, naturalistic settings. To study this, I use an immersive virtual foraging task in which people freely explore a valley of hundreds of bushes in continuous space and time, harvesting fruit for reward. Reward depends on multiple continuously valued features (canopy shape, fruit color, and terrain moisture), so participants must learn this mapping while deciding, in real time, whether to explore unfamiliar bushes, exploit known ones, or move on. I analyze where, when, and how people harvest to characterize their learning and decision making strategies. In parallel, I am building a family of computational agents to perform the task, from simple heuristics to learners with high-dimensional feature embeddings, memory replay, and exploration strategies, and I plan to implement the best-fitting ones as spiking neural networks.
