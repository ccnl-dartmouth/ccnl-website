---
title: Computational principles and neural mechanisms of flexible learning and decision making in naturalistic settings
order: 1
---
Learning from reward feedback in naturalistic settings is particularly challenging because environments are constantly changing and often present **multiple choice options** [@2021-soltani-timescales-cognition-brain, @2022-soltani-computational-models-adaptive]. When confronted with many options, each defined by multiple attributes such as color or shape, we face the **curse of dimensionality**: the number of possible combinations grows rapidly as more attributes are added, making it increasingly difficult to learn about every option from experience.

Our work has established that learning and combining the predictive values of individual features (feature-based learning) can reduce dimensionality, enabling faster learning without sacrificing precision [@2017-farashahi-feature-based-learning, @2018-farashahi-influence-learning-strategy, @2020-farashahi-learning-arbitrary-stimulus, @2021-farashahi-computational-mechanisms-distributed, @2025-wang-contributions-attention-learning, @2024-yazdanpanah-contribution-statistical-learning]. We have shown that representations supporting this learning depend on environmental stability, progressing in stable environments from the values of individual features to combinations of features and ultimately to individual options. We have demonstrated these patterns behaviorally in humans and used recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to model human learning, revealing transitions among different learning strategies and candidate neural mechanisms [@2021-farashahi-computational-mechanisms-distributed].

In addition to choosing among many options and features [@2012-soltani-range-normalization-model, @2019-spitmaan-combinations-low-level], decision making in naturalistic settings requires integrating information from multiple sources while learning from feedback that often does not specify which source, feature, or action was responsible for the outcome. The brain must therefore solve the **credit assignment** problem [@2010-soltani-synaptic-computation-underlying, @2016-soltani-neural-substrates-cognitive]: determining what should receive credit or blame for the observed reward. Our recent work on this line of research reveals the role of attention [@2025-wang-contributions-attention-learning, @2026-shahamati-value-guided-attention].
